## Slide 4 — Red light, drive, red light again

Picture driving down a city road: you pass one green light and then hit red at every light after it. On our default road, with the lights not coordinated, an average car waits 86.5 seconds and stops 4 times on a 5-light trip. That wasted time, fuel and braking is the problem the project solves. On the website, the 'Try it' page headline says 'waits 14 s instead of 86 s': the 86 is this number.

## Slide 5 — Why it happens: every light turns green together

This is the core of the problem. The timing bars show 3 minutes of five lights that all start green at the same moment. Driving between lights takes 36 seconds, but the green only lasts 30 seconds, so a car always arrives just after its light turned red. The 'How it works' page opens with exactly this explanation, using your road's numbers.

## Slide 6 — The fix: delay each light's green (a green wave)

The fix is not to change how long lights stay green, only WHEN each one starts. That start time, measured from Light 1, is called the offset. One offset per light is a timing plan. The 'Try it' page shows this plan as animated lights and a table that compares each offset with the 36 s driving time. Why +40 and not +36? Because cars drive in both directions and the plan has to suit both; slide 7 explains why that is hard.

## Slide 7 — Why we need AI: too many plans to check by hand

This slide is the bridge to AI. Each light has 12 possible offsets, so five lights give 20,736 different plans, and seven lights give almost 3 million. On top of that, the two directions want different plans and every light affects the next. We want the BEST plan and a PROOF that it is best, which is exactly what classical AI search gives us.

## Slide 9 — Classical AI vs machine learning

People often hear 'AI' and think of ChatGPT or self-driving cars, which use machine learning. Foundations of AI starts earlier: with problems you can describe exactly, solved by searching through possibilities in a smart way. Our project is entirely classical AI. There is no training data and no randomness in the answer: the same road always gives the same plan.

## Slide 10 — Our project as an AI agent (PEAS)

PEAS is the standard way the textbook (Russell and Norvig) describes an AI agent. Our agent's goal is the lowest score. Its world is the simulated road. Its only action is choosing the offsets. What it 'sees' is the simulator's result for each plan. Say clearly that it plans offline: it does not control real lights.

## Slide 11 — What kind of environment is it?

These six words come straight from the textbook's chapter on environments. Real traffic is messier (partially observable, random), but our simulated road is fully observable, deterministic, static, discrete and known. That simplification is what lets us use exact search and claim 'proven best'. Slide 31 lists what we gave up.

## Slide 13 — Constraint satisfaction problem (CSP) words

A CSP, constraint satisfaction problem, is the main AI idea in the project. It has variables (the offsets), domains (the 12 allowed values each) and constraints (the green-wave rule). We add an objective, the score, so we are not just looking for ANY allowed plan but the best one; textbooks call this a constraint optimisation problem. The right column is the plain word used on the website's glossary.

## Slide 14 — Search words

Search means exploring possibilities in an organised way. Picture a tree: the root is an empty plan, the first level picks Light 2's offset, the next level Light 3's, and so on. A leaf is a complete plan. Backtracking walks this tree depth-first. Complete means it never misses a solution; optimal means the answer it returns is the best.

## Slide 15 — Pruning and strategy words

Pruning is what makes search smart rather than brute force: whole branches of the tree are cut off without being explored. Forward checking cuts values that break the rule. Branch and bound cuts branches that cannot beat the best plan found so far, using an estimate called a bound. If the estimate never over-estimates it is admissible, which guarantees we never cut the true best plan.

## Slide 17 — The pipeline: what happens when you press the button

This is how AI is integrated. You describe a road on the Try it page. The program creates a fixed set of cars, runs the three strategies, and every candidate plan is scored by the same simulator. The CSP search is the AI that chooses the plan; the simulator is how it measures plans; the verifier independently checks the answer. The file names are in src/traffic_coordination/.

## Slide 18 — Our problem written as a CSP

This is the formal CSP. Four variables, twelve values each, one rule per pair of neighbouring lights, and an objective. The rule says each light's offset should be close (within 10 s) to the previous light's offset plus the driving time, which is what a green wave would be. It keeps plans sensible and the search small: 256 allowed plans instead of 20,736.

## Slide 19 — How a plan is scored (the simulator)

The simulator is the environment model, the 'rules of the world' the agent uses to judge a plan. It follows these five rules. The score combines total waiting and total stops. Checking the arithmetic: uncoordinated cars wait 3,460 seconds in total with 160 stops, giving 3,780. The optimized plan has 540 seconds and 39 stops, giving 618.

## Slide 20 — Strategies 1 and 2: the comparisons

We compare against two simple strategies. 'No coordination' is the baseline. The 'simple green wave' is a greedy heuristic used by traffic engineers: it is fast and good for one direction, but it ignores cars going the other way. On one test road (H1) the greedy plan was even worse than no coordination. The website works through the greedy arithmetic with your own road.

## Slide 21 — Strategy 3, step 1: backtracking search

Backtracking is depth-first search over the tree of choices. Only 4 values appear per level here because forward checking (next slide) has already crossed out the others. The 'Under the hood' page draws the real search: a chart of which light is being timed at each step. Climbing means going deeper; dropping back means backtracking.

## Slide 22 — Step 2: forward checking crosses out bad values early

Forward checking looks one step ahead. As soon as one light gets a value, it removes values from the next light that would break the green-wave rule. Our constraint only links neighbouring lights, so checking the next light is enough. On the default road it crossed out 680 values: 8 values each time one of the 85 partial plans was extended. This is real pruning, and the website prints the count for your road.

## Slide 23 — Step 3: branch and bound, and an honest zero

Branch and bound is implemented and counted, but with the bound h = 0 it never prunes. That is correct (an admissible bound keeps the answer optimal) but weak. We report the zero honestly; expect a viva question on it. Answer: h = 0 is admissible and consistent, so correctness holds, but a lower bound of 0 can never beat a real incumbent, so nothing is skipped. A better bound is future work.

## Slide 24 — Is it really the best? Double-checking the answer

We never just claim the answer is the best. The search itself proves it when it finishes, and an independently written brute-force verifier double-checks it whenever the number of plans is small enough (up to 250,000). In the benchmarks it agreed on 12 of 14 roads; the other 2 were too large and are reported as skipped, not as verified.

## Slide 26 — The default road: 84% less waiting

This is the Try it page on the default road. An average car now waits 13.5 s instead of 86.5 s and stops about once instead of four times. The green badge means the search finished and proved the plan is the best under the rules.

## Slide 27 — Seeing the difference: time-space diagrams

Time-space diagrams are the traffic engineer's standard picture. A straight slope means cars are moving; a flat segment means they are stopped. Without coordination both bunches stop at nearly every light. With the optimized plan both directions pass almost without stopping.

## Slide 28 — Across five test roads, the search always wins or ties

These five roads were run ahead of time with the benchmark script (experiments/results). Each changes one thing from the default road. On road A the green wave is already optimal, so it ties. Roads C and E have too many plans for brute force, so their proof comes from the search running to completion with the green-wave rule on. The Experiments page also lets you run a live comparison.
