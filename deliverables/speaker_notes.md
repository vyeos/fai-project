## Slide 1 — Timing traffic lights with classical AI

This deck is meant to be read next to the website. Every slide has a blue tag in the top right that tells you which page and section of the dashboard shows the same idea. Open the dashboard (python -m streamlit run app.py) and keep it beside the slides.

## Slide 2 — Why it happens: every light turns green together

This is the core of the problem. The timing bars show 3 minutes of five lights that all start green at the same moment. Driving between lights takes 36 seconds, but the green only lasts 30 seconds, so a car always arrives just after its light turned red. The 'How it works' page opens with exactly this explanation, using your road's numbers.

## Slide 3 — The fix: delay each light's green (a green wave)

The fix is not to change how long lights stay green, only WHEN each one starts. That start time, measured from Light 1, is called the offset. One offset per light is a timing plan. The 'Try it' page shows this plan as animated lights and a table that compares each offset with the 36 s driving time. Why +40 and not +36? Because cars drive in both directions and the plan has to suit both; the next slide explains why that is hard.

## Slide 4 — Why we need AI: too many plans to check by hand

This slide is the bridge to AI. Each light has 12 possible offsets, so five lights give 20,736 different plans, and seven lights give almost 3 million. On top of that, the two directions want different plans and every light affects the next. We want the BEST plan and a PROOF that it is best, which is exactly what classical AI search gives us.

## Slide 5 — What kind of AI this is

People hear 'AI' and think of ChatGPT, which is machine learning. Foundations of AI starts with problems you can describe exactly, solved by searching through possibilities in a smart way. Our project is entirely classical AI. PEAS is the textbook's way to describe an agent: its goal is the lowest score, its world is the simulated road, its only action is choosing offsets, and what it 'sees' is the simulator's result. It plans offline; it does not control real lights. The six environment words explain why exact search works here: real traffic is random and partly hidden, our simulated road is not.

## Slide 6 — FAI words, and what the website calls them

This one table is the vocabulary for the whole project. A CSP, constraint satisfaction problem, has variables (the offsets), domains (12 allowed values each) and constraints (the green-wave rule); we add an objective, the score, so we want the best allowed plan. Backtracking walks the tree of choices, forward checking and branch and bound prune it. The right column is the plain word the website uses, so you can find each idea there.

## Slide 7 — The pipeline: what happens when you press the button

This is how AI is integrated. You describe a road on the Try it page. The program creates a fixed set of cars, runs the three strategies, and every candidate plan is scored by the same simulator. The CSP search is the AI that chooses the plan; the simulator is how it measures plans; the verifier independently checks the answer. The file names are in src/traffic_coordination/.

## Slide 8 — Our problem written as a CSP

This is the formal CSP. Four variables, twelve values each, one rule per pair of neighbouring lights, and an objective. The rule says each light's offset should be close (within 10 s) to the previous light's offset plus the driving time, which is what a green wave would be. It keeps plans sensible and the search small: 256 allowed plans instead of 20,736.

## Slide 9 — Strategies 1 and 2: the comparisons

We compare against two simple strategies. 'No coordination' is the baseline. The 'simple green wave' is a greedy heuristic used by traffic engineers: it is fast and good for one direction, but it ignores cars going the other way. On one test road (H1) the greedy plan was even worse than no coordination. The website works through the greedy arithmetic with your own road.

## Slide 10 — Strategy 3, step 1: backtracking search

Backtracking is depth-first search over the tree of choices. Only 4 values appear per level here because forward checking (next slide) has already crossed out the others. The 'Under the hood' page draws the real search: a chart of which light is being timed at each step. Climbing means going deeper; dropping back means backtracking.

## Slide 11 — Step 2: forward checking crosses out bad values early

Forward checking looks one step ahead. As soon as one light gets a value, it removes values from the next light that would break the green-wave rule. Our constraint only links neighbouring lights, so checking the next light is enough. On the default road it crossed out 680 values: 8 values each time one of the 85 partial plans was extended. This is real pruning, and the website prints the count for your road.

## Slide 12 — Step 3: branch and bound, and checking the answer

Branch and bound is implemented and counted, but with the bound h = 0 it never prunes. That is correct (an admissible bound keeps the answer optimal) but weak, and we report the zero honestly; a sharper bound is future work. We never just claim the answer is the best: the finished search proves it, and an independently written brute-force verifier double-checks it whenever there are at most 250,000 plans.

## Slide 13 — The default road: 84% less waiting

This is the Try it page on the default road. An average car now waits 13.5 s instead of 86.5 s and stops about once instead of four times. The time-space diagrams are the traffic engineer's standard picture: a slope means moving, a flat segment means stopped. The green badge means the search finished and proved the plan is the best under the rules.

## Slide 14 — Across five test roads, the search always wins or ties

These five roads were run ahead of time with the benchmark script (experiments/results). Each changes one thing from the default road. On road A the green wave is already optimal, so it ties. Roads C and E have too many plans for brute force, so their proof comes from the search running to completion with the green-wave rule on. The Experiments page also lets you run a live comparison.

## Slide 15 — Limits, and the whole project in one minute

Say the limitations before you are asked: the numbers are simulated under simplifications, so they compare plans fairly but are not field predictions. Then the one-minute summary. Likely viva questions: Where is the AI? (the CSP search). Why not try every plan? (it grows x12 per light; pruning keeps it to 256). How do you know it is the best? (complete search, brute force agrees). Why does branch and bound prune 0? (h = 0 is admissible but weak). Why not machine learning? (rules are known, no data). More in docs/viva_questions.md.
